Puerto Rican Spanish is quick, musical, and unmistakable. **Puerto Rican Spanish slang** borrows from English, softens its r sounds, and turns everyday chat into something closer to a song. Learn a handful of these words and a conversation in San Juan, Ponce, or Mayagüez will open up fast.

Below are 20 **Puerto Rican Spanish slang words and expressions** locals really use. For each one you get the meaning, an example in context, and the English translation, so you know exactly how and when to say it.

## Puerto Rican Slang for Greetings and People

This is how boricuas greet each other and talk about the people around them.

### 1. Wepa

**Meaning**: Yes! Woohoo! A shout of pure joy, celebration, or approval. Probably the most exported Puerto Rican word of all.

**Example**: "¡Wepa, ganamos!" ("Woohoo, we won!")

### 2. Acho / chacho

**Meaning**: Man, geez. A filler interjection shortened from *muchacho*, dropped at the start of a sentence to add feeling.

**Example**: "Acho, qué calor hace hoy." ("Man, it's so hot today.")

### 3. Ay bendito

**Meaning**: Oh dear, poor thing. The most Puerto Rican expression there is. It carries sympathy, pity, tenderness, or mild exasperation depending on the tone.

**Example**: "¡Ay bendito, se le perdió el celular!" ("Oh dear, he lost his phone!")

### 4. Boricua

**Meaning**: A Puerto Rican. It comes from *Borikén*, the Taíno name for the island, and it is a badge of pride, not just a nationality.

**Example**: "Soy boricua de pura cepa." ("I'm a Puerto Rican through and through.")

### 5. Corillo

**Meaning**: Your crew, your group of friends, the people you always roll with.

**Example**: "Salimos con el corillo el viernes." ("We're going out with the crew on Friday.")

## Puerto Rican Slang for Reactions and Opinions

These are the words boricuas reach for when something is great, terrible, or just too much.

### 6. Brutal

**Meaning**: Awesome, amazing, incredible. In Puerto Rico the word flips completely and becomes the highest praise.

**Example**: "Ese concierto estuvo brutal." ("That concert was amazing.")

### 7. Nítido

**Meaning**: Cool, great, all good. A quick way to approve of something or agree to a plan.

**Example**: "¿Nos vemos a las siete? Nítido." ("See you at seven? Cool.")

### 8. Revolú

**Meaning**: A mess, chaos, a commotion. It works for a messy room and for a full blown scandal.

**Example**: "Se formó un revolú en la fiesta." ("A big commotion broke out at the party.")

### 9. Enfogonao

**Meaning**: Furious, fuming. It comes from *fogón*, the cooking fire, so the image is of someone burning up.

**Example**: "Está enfogonao porque no lo llamaron." ("He's furious because they didn't call him.")

### 10. Cafre

**Meaning**: Tacky, trashy, without class. A strong word, used for behavior, clothes, or music taste.

**Example**: "Esa combinación de colores es bien cafre." ("That color combination is really tacky.")

### 11. Charro

**Meaning**: Lame, corny, ridiculous. Nothing to do with the Mexican charro. In Puerto Rico it means something is embarrassingly unfunny.

**Example**: "Ese chiste está charro." ("That joke is lame.")

## Puerto Rican Slang for Everyday Life

The verbs and expressions that fill an ordinary boricua day.

### 12. Janguear

**Meaning**: To hang out. Straight from the English "hang out", conjugated like any Spanish verb.

**Example**: "Vamos a janguear en la playa." ("Let's hang out at the beach.")

### 13. Bregar

**Meaning**: To deal with something, to handle it, to sort it out. A true Puerto Rican workhorse verb that covers work, problems, and relationships.

**Example**: "Tengo que bregar con eso mañana." ("I have to deal with that tomorrow.")

### 14. Pichear

**Meaning**: To ignore someone, to leave them on read. Borrowed from the baseball "pitch".

**Example**: "Le escribí y me picheó." ("I texted him and he ignored me.")

### 15. Gufear

**Meaning**: To joke around, to mess about. From the English "goof".

**Example**: "No te enojes, estaba gufeando." ("Don't get mad, I was just joking.")

### 16. Chinchorrear

**Meaning**: To go from one *chinchorro* to another, the small roadside spots that serve local food and cold drinks. It is a weekend institution.

**Example**: "El domingo nos fuimos a chinchorrear por la montaña." ("On Sunday we went bar hopping up the mountain.")

### 17. Dar pon

**Meaning**: To give someone a ride. *Pedir pon* is to ask for one.

**Example**: "¿Me das pon hasta el trabajo?" ("Can you give me a ride to work?")

### 18. Chavos

**Meaning**: Money. Not coins specifically, just money in general.

**Example**: "No tengo chavos hasta el viernes." ("I don't have any money until Friday.")

### 19. Mangó bajito

**Meaning**: An easy target, or something far too easy. The image is a mango hanging low enough to grab without effort.

**Example**: "Ese examen fue un mangó bajito." ("That exam was a piece of cake.")

### 20. Al garete

**Meaning**: Out of control, adrift, all over the place. It comes from sailing, where a boat *al garete* has lost its steering.

**Example**: "Ese proyecto va al garete." ("That project is going off the rails.")

## What Makes Puerto Rican Spanish Sound So Different

The slang is only half the story. A few habits of the accent explain why boricua Spanish is so easy to recognize and, at first, so hard to follow:

- **The r that turns into an l.** *Puerto Rico* often comes out as "Puelto Rico", and *verdad* as "veldad". It is a hallmark of the island, especially outside San Juan.
- **The disappearing s.** *Los amigos* becomes "loh amigoh". Once your ear accepts that the s is breathed rather than pronounced, comprehension jumps.
- **English borrowed and conjugated.** *Janguear*, *pichear*, *gufear*, and *parquear* all take an English verb and run it through Spanish grammar. This is not lazy Spanish, it is a century of shared history doing its work.
- **Diminutives everywhere.** *Ahorita*, *cerquita*, *poquito*. They soften a sentence and make it friendlier, and boricuas use them constantly.

## How to Use Puerto Rican Slang Naturally

To go from reading these words to sounding like a real boricua:

1. **Start with the warm ones.** *Wepa*, *acho*, *ay bendito*, and *nítido* are friendly, safe, and instantly Puerto Rican. Lead with those and the rest will follow.
2. **Respect the strong ones.** *Cafre* and *charro* are judgments about taste, and they sting. Keep them for close friends, or for laughing at yourself.
