Ask a Brazilian for advice and you will often get a proverb instead of a lecture. **Brazilian Portuguese proverbs** are short, rhythmic and unusually practical, and they show up everywhere: in a grandmother's kitchen, in a WhatsApp group, in the middle of a football argument.

Below are 20 **Brazilian Portuguese proverbs about life with English translations**. For each one you get the Portuguese original, a literal translation, and the meaning Brazilians actually give it, so you understand not just the words but the moment when someone would say them.

## Why Proverbs Carry So Much Weight in Brazil

Brazilian Portuguese inherited most of these sayings from Portugal, then reshaped them. The images changed to fit the country: monkeys instead of foxes, hens and fishing instead of European farm life, food metaphors everywhere. What survived the crossing was the function. A proverb lets you give hard advice without sounding superior, because the wisdom belongs to everyone rather than to you.

That is why you hear them from people of every age and education level, and why they turn up in newspaper headlines and song lyrics as often as in family conversation. For a learner they are also efficient: one proverb teaches you a full sentence with natural word order, real vocabulary, and a cultural attitude attached. Recognizing the first four words is usually enough, because Brazilians often trail off and let you finish the rest.

## Proverbs About Patience and Persistence

Brazilians have a deep vocabulary for the slow way of getting things done.

### 1. Água mole em pedra dura tanto bate até que fura.

**Literal**: Soft water on hard stone strikes until it makes a hole.
**Meaning**: Persistence wears down any obstacle. It is the proverb Brazilians reach for when someone is close to giving up.

### 2. De grão em grão, a galinha enche o papo.

**Literal**: Grain by grain, the hen fills her crop.
**Meaning**: Small steady amounts add up. Used for saving money, learning a language, or any progress that feels too slow.

### 3. Quem espera sempre alcança.

**Literal**: Who waits always reaches.
**Meaning**: Patience gets you there in the end. Said as comfort to someone stuck waiting for news.

### 4. A pressa é inimiga da perfeição.

**Literal**: Haste is the enemy of perfection.
**Meaning**: Rush the work and the work suffers. The standard reply to anyone cutting corners.

### 5. Deus ajuda quem cedo madruga.

**Literal**: God helps the one who rises early.
**Meaning**: The early riser gets the reward. Brazil's version of the early bird, with a religious accent that is very common in everyday speech.

## Proverbs About People and Character

Half of Brazilian wisdom is really about reading other people.

### 6. Diga-me com quem andas e te direi quem és.

**Literal**: Tell me who you walk with and I will tell you who you are.
**Meaning**: You are judged by your company. Often said to a parent worrying about a teenager's friends.

### 7. Filho de peixe, peixinho é.

**Literal**: The child of a fish is a little fish.
**Meaning**: Like father, like son. Said with affection when a kid clearly inherited a parent's talent or stubbornness.

### 8. Pau que nasce torto morre torto.

**Literal**: A stick born crooked dies crooked.
**Meaning**: People rarely change their nature. Usually said with a resigned shrug about someone who keeps repeating the same mistake.

### 9. Macaco velho não põe a mão em cumbuca.

**Literal**: An old monkey does not put his hand in a pot.
**Meaning**: Experience makes you cautious. An old hand does not fall for an obvious trap.

**Literal**: Each monkey on its own branch.

## Proverbs About Luck, Fate and Consequences

### 12. Um dia é da caça, o outro é do caçador.

**Literal**: One day belongs to the prey, the next to the hunter.
**Meaning**: Fortunes reverse. Said when someone on top today will be on the bottom tomorrow, and Brazilians say it a lot about football.

### 13. Quem com ferro fere, com ferro será ferido.

**Literal**: Who wounds with iron shall be wounded with iron.
**Meaning**: What you do comes back to you. The Brazilian statement of poetic justice.

### 14. Quem semeia vento colhe tempestade.

**Literal**: Who sows wind reaps a storm.
**Meaning**: Small acts of trouble grow into big consequences. Used for arguments that were allowed to escalate.

### 15. Onde há fumaça, há fogo.

**Literal**: Where there is smoke, there is fire.
**Meaning**: Rumors usually rest on something real. Said when everyone suspects the same thing.

### 16. Quem não arrisca, não petisca.

**Literal**: Who does not risk does not snack.
**Meaning**: Nothing ventured, nothing gained. The food metaphor is pure Brazil, and it makes the advice sound friendly rather than harsh.

## Proverbs About Making Do

Brazil calls the art of improvising a solution *jeitinho*, and several proverbs live in that world.

### 17. Quem não tem cão caça com gato.

**Literal**: Who has no dog hunts with a cat.
**Meaning**: Use whatever you have. Probably the single most Brazilian proverb on this list.

### 18. Casa de ferreiro, espeto de pau.

**Literal**: Blacksmith's house, wooden spit.
**Meaning**: The expert never applies their skill at home. The mechanic with the broken car, the chef who eats instant noodles.

### 19. O barato sai caro.

**Literal**: The cheap turns out expensive.
**Meaning**: Buying the cheapest option costs you more later. Said after someone regrets a bargain.

### 20. Nem tudo que reluz é ouro.

**Literal**: Not everything that shines is gold.
**Meaning**: Appearances mislead. Applied to job offers, deals and people who look too good to be true.

## How to Start Using These Proverbs

Reading a proverb and landing one in conversation are different skills. Three things help:

1. **Pick four, not twenty.** Choose the ones that match how you already think. *De grão em grão* fits a patient person; *quem não arrisca, não petisca* fits someone else entirely.
2. **Watch the register.** These belong to relaxed, warm conversation. In a formal meeting they sound folksy, which is sometimes exactly the effect you want.
