Salvadorans call themselves *guanacos*, and **Salvadoran Spanish slang** is quick, direct and unmistakably its own thing. It shares a few words with Guatemala and Honduras, but the way Salvadorans string them together, always with *vos*, always with a shortcut, is what makes it recognisable within three sentences.

Below are 20 **Salvadoran Spanish slang words and expressions** you will hear daily in San Salvador, at a *pupusería*, or in any conversation between *cheros*. Each entry has the meaning, an example, and the English translation.

## How Salvadorans Talk About Each Other

### 1. Guanaco / guanaca
A Salvadoran. Used with pride among Salvadorans themselves, and it beats *salvadoreño* in casual speech.

*Somos guanacos, aquí se come pupusa los domingos.* (We're Salvadoran, here we eat pupusas on Sundays.)

### 2. Chero / chera
Friend, buddy. This is the single most Salvadoran word on the list, and you will hear it in every other sentence.

*Voy a salir con mis cheros el viernes.* (I'm going out with my friends on Friday.)

### 3. Maje
Dude, guy. Between friends it is neutral and constant. Said with the wrong tone it means fool, so the tone carries the meaning.

*Ese maje siempre llega tarde.* (That guy is always late.)

### 4. Bicho / bicha
A kid or a teenager. Elsewhere in Latin America *bicho* means something very different, so this one is worth filing under "El Salvador only".

*Los bichos están jugando en el patio.* (The kids are playing in the yard.)

### 5. Cipote / cipota
Another word for a child, slightly more affectionate and used by older speakers.

*Cuando era cipote vivía en Santa Ana.* (When I was a kid I lived in Santa Ana.)

### 6. Chele / chela
Light skinned or fair haired. Descriptive, extremely common, and often used as a nickname.

*La chele que trabaja en la farmacia me ayudó.* (The fair haired woman who works at the pharmacy helped me.)

### 7. Baboso / babosa
A fool, someone doing something silly. Softer than it looks on paper, and used with affection among friends.

*No seás baboso, eso no se hace así.* (Don't be silly, that's not how you do it.)

## Everyday Salvadoran Expressions

### 8. Chivo
Cool, great, excellent. The default word of approval in El Salvador.

*Está bien chivo tu celular nuevo.* (Your new phone is really cool.)

### 9. Púchica
Surprise, frustration or sympathy, all in one word, and always safe to say out loud.

*Púchica, se fue la luz otra vez.* (Oh man, the power went out again.)

### 10. Cabal
Exactly, right, spot on. Salvadorans use it as agreement the way other Spanish speakers use *exacto*.

*Cabal, eso mismo estaba pensando.* (Exactly, that's just what I was thinking.)

### 11. Trucha
Sharp, alert, on the ball. *Ponerse trucha* is to pay attention.

*Ponete trucha con la mochila en el bus.* (Keep an eye on your backpack on the bus.)

### 12. Pisto
Money. Shared with Guatemala and just as common here.

*Ando sin pisto hasta el día de pago.* (I'm broke until payday.)

### 13. Chambre
Gossip. *Echar chambre* is to gossip, and *chambroso* is the person who does it.

*No me gusta el chambre de la oficina.* (I don't like the office gossip.)

## Salvadoran Slang for Things and Situations

### 14. Volado
A thing, a matter, a favour, an errand. Beautifully vague and endlessly useful.

*Tengo un volado que hacer antes de las cinco.* (I have something to take care of before five.)

### 15. Chunche
An object whose name you cannot remember, or cannot be bothered to say.

*Pasame ese chunche que está en la mesa.* (Pass me that thing on the table.)

### 16. Bayunco
Silly, clownish, someone who plays the fool to make people laugh.

*Tu primo es bien bayunco en las fiestas.* (Your cousin is a real clown at parties.)

### 17. Bolo
Drunk. Same meaning across much of Central America.

*No manejés, andás bolo.* (Don't drive, you're drunk.)

### 18. Guaro
Hard liquor, usually cane spirit. The word covers the drink and the whole evening built around it.

*Compraron guaro para la celebración.* (They bought liquor for the celebration.)

### 19. Vergón
Great, awesome. Strong and informal, so it belongs with friends rather than at work.

*Estuvo vergón el concierto.* (The concert was awesome.)

### 20. Qué onda vos
What's up. The standard Salvadoran greeting, and the clearest three word summary of how the country speaks: a question, a *vos*, and no wasted syllables.

*Qué onda vos, cómo te fue en el trabajo?* (What's up, how did work go?)

## Five More You Will Hear Within a Day

These did not make the main twenty, but they turn up constantly and they are worth recognising.

**Chuco.** Dirty. It is also the name of a warm, dark corn drink sold at street stalls, so context decides whether someone is insulting your kitchen or offering you breakfast.

**A la orden.** The standard reply to thank you, and also what a shopkeeper says to invite you in. It replaces *de nada* almost entirely.

**Salú.** Cheers, and also what you say when someone sneezes. Short, warm, everywhere.

**Andar hule.** To be completely broke, with *hule* meaning rubber, as in worn down to nothing.

**Pupusear.** To go out for pupusas. The national dish is common enough to have generated its own verb, which tells you most of what you need to know about El Salvador.

## How to Use Salvadoran Slang Without Sounding Off

**Use *vos*, not *tú*.** Salvadorans use *vos* almost universally in informal speech, with its own verb forms: *vos sos*, *vos tenés*, *vos venís*, and the imperative *vení*, *decime*, *ponete*. This is the backbone of sounding Salvadoran.

**Expect dropped letters.** *Para* becomes *pa*, *está* becomes *tá*, and final *s* often softens. Do not try to force it, but do train your ear for it, or you will miss half of what is said.

**Mind the regional traps.** *Bicho* means a child here and something coarse in other countries. Slang is only local by definition, and a word that works in San Salvador can land badly in Lima or San Juan.

**Watch the tone with *maje*.** The same word is a friendly "dude" and a flat insult depending entirely on how it is said. When in doubt, use *chero*.
