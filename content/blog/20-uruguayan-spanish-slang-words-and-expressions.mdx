Learners who arrive in Montevideo after studying in Buenos Aires get a small surprise. The grammar is the same, the *vos* is the same, the accent is close, and then someone calls their sneakers *championes* and ends every second sentence with *bo*. **Uruguayan Spanish slang** overlaps heavily with Argentinian slang, but it is not a copy of it, and the differences are exactly the words you will hear fifty times a day.

This guide covers 20 slang words and expressions used in Uruguay, with the meaning and an example for each. It is organised in two halves: the ones that are distinctly Uruguayan, and the Rioplatense vocabulary that Uruguay shares with Argentina. Knowing which is which saves you from sounding like a tourist who studied the wrong country.

## Slang You Will Hear in Uruguay and Almost Nowhere Else

### 1. Bo

The single most Uruguayan word there is. It is a vocative, roughly "hey" or "dude", dropped at the start or the end of a sentence to grab attention or add emphasis. Argentinians use *che*; Uruguayans use both, but *bo* is theirs.

**Example**:
"Bo, ¿vamos a la playa?"
("Hey, shall we go to the beach?")

### 2. Ta

A shortened *está*, used on its own to mean "OK", "fine", "got it", or "enough". You will hear it as a full reply, sometimes twice in a row.

**Example**:
"Nos vemos a las ocho." "Ta."
("See you at eight." "OK.")

### 3. Gurí, gurisa

A child or a young person. The word comes from Guaraní and is common in Uruguay and the Argentinian northeast, but a Montevidean uses it far more than a porteño.

**Example**:
"Ese gurí juega muy bien."
("That kid plays really well.")

### 4. Botija

Another word for a kid or a young person, this one specifically Uruguayan. It is affectionate rather than dismissive.

**Example**:
"De botija iba todos los veranos a Rocha."
("As a kid I went to Rocha every summer.")

### 5. Championes

Sneakers, trainers, running shoes. The word comes from an old shoe brand and it is one of the fastest ways to place a speaker in Uruguay. Argentinians say *zapatillas*.

**Example**:
"Me compré championes nuevos."
("I bought new sneakers.")

### 6. Ñeri

A friend, a mate. Informal and warm, used mostly among younger speakers.

**Example**:
"Vino con dos ñeris del liceo."
("He came with two friends from school.")

### 7. Pila

A lot, plenty. Used as a quantifier before a noun, and also on its own to mean "a lot".

**Example**:
"Había pila de gente en el centro."
("There were loads of people downtown.")

### 8. ¡Pah!

An interjection of surprise or admiration, somewhere between "wow" and "whoa". Short, loud, and very Uruguayan.

**Example**:
"¡Pah! No sabía que cocinabas así."
("Whoa, I didn't know you could cook like this.")

### 9. Bizcochos

Not a slang word exactly, but a vocabulary trap worth knowing. In Uruguay *bizcochos* are the sweet and savoury pastries you buy for breakfast, not biscuits or cookies. Ordering them correctly is a small local test you can pass on day one.

**Example**:
"Traé bizcochos para el mate."
("Bring pastries for the mate.")

### 10. Cuadro

Your football team. Where other countries say *equipo*, Uruguay says *cuadro*, and the question comes up quickly.

**Example**:
"¿De qué cuadro sos?"
("Which team do you support?")

### 11. Salado

Literally "salty". It means impressive, intense, or difficult, depending on the tone. A concert can be *salado* and so can an exam.

**Example**:
"El partido estuvo salado."
("The match was intense.")

### 12. Garra charrúa

Literally "Charrúa grit", after the Indigenous Charrúa people. It names the stubborn, never-surrender spirit Uruguayans claim as a national trait, usually in football but not only there. You will meet this phrase in conversation and in every sports headline.

**Example**:
"Ganaron con pura garra charrúa."
("They won on pure grit.")

### 13. Vo

The pronoun *vos*, with the final *s* softened or dropped, the way it actually sounds in Montevideo. Written down it looks like a typo. Spoken, it is the default.

**Example**:
"¿Y vo qué decís?"
("And what do you say?")

### 14. Estar en la vuelta

To be around, to be nearby or available. Useful and very common.

**Example**:
"Si estás en la vuelta, pasá por casa."
("If you are around, drop by.")

### 15. Pichi

Scruffy, low rent, cheap looking. It describes a person, a place, or a thing, and it is not a compliment, so recognise it rather than use it.

**Example**:
"El lugar estaba medio pichi."
("The place was a bit shabby.")

## Rioplatense Slang Uruguay Shares With Argentina

These cross the river freely. You will hear them on both banks, so they are worth learning, but they will not mark you as speaking Uruguayan.

### 16. Che

The classic Rioplatense attention grabber, "hey". Uruguayans use it alongside *bo*, often in the same sentence.

**Example**:
"Che, ¿me pasás el mate?"
("Hey, can you pass me the mate?")

### 17. Laburo

Work or job, from the Italian *lavoro*, one of many lunfardo words that entered Rioplatense Spanish through Italian immigration.

**Example**:
"Salgo del laburo a las seis."
("I finish work at six.")

### 18. Copado

Cool, great, enjoyable. Applies to people, plans, and places alike.

**Example**:
"Tu hermano es re copado."
("Your brother is really cool.")

### 19. Careta

Literally "mask". It describes someone fake or two faced, a person who performs a version of themselves that is not real.

**Example**:
"No seas careta, decí lo que pensás."
("Don't be fake, say what you think.")

### 20. Ni ahí

Literally "not even there". It means "no way" or "not a chance", and it is one of the most useful refusals in the region.

**Example**:
"¿Vas a la reunión?" "Ni ahí."
("Are you going to the meeting?" "No chance.")

## Why Uruguayan Slang Is Not Just Argentinian Slang

Uruguay and Argentina share the Rioplatense variety: the same *vos* conjugations, the same Italian influenced intonation, the same lunfardo inheritance. That is why so much vocabulary crosses the river without changing. But two countries that share a grammar do not share a life, and the vocabulary splits exactly where daily life does.

The clearest split is in everyday objects. Sneakers are *championes* in Montevideo and *zapatillas* in Buenos Aires. A football team is a *cuadro* in Uruguay and an *equipo* almost everywhere else. Breakfast pastries are *bizcochos*, a word an Argentinian will understand but not use the same way. None of these are slang in the rebellious sense, they are simply the local name for a thing, and they are the words you need on your first morning.

The second split is in the fillers. *Bo* and *ta* carry no dictionary meaning worth the name, and yet they are the two words a Uruguayan says most. This is generally how it works with regional speech: the vocabulary that identifies a place is rarely the vocabulary that carries the information.

## How to Use This Without Sounding Like a Textbook

Slang goes wrong when it is deployed too early or too heavily. A few things that work:

- Start with the connectors, not the nouns. *Ta*, *bo* and *pila* are low risk and they change how natural you sound immediately. *Pichi* and *careta* carry judgement, so understand them before you say them.
- Say *vos* forms properly. Uruguayan slang sitting on top of *tú* conjugations sounds stranger than no slang at all.
- Pick one register and hold it. Mixing Uruguayan words into a Mexican accent is the fastest way to sound like a language app.
