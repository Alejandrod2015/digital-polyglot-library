German has a proverb for almost every situation, and most of them are about doing the job properly. Where Spanish reaches for food and French for love, German reaches for work, punctuality and the cost of doing things badly. Learning **German proverbs with English translations** is one of the fastest ways to hear how the language actually thinks.

This guide covers 25 of them, split between the ones about work and the ones about life, with the literal translation, the real meaning and an example for each.

## Proverbs About Work and Effort

### 1. Morgenstund hat Gold im Mund

**Literal**: The morning hour has gold in its mouth.
**Meaning**: The early bird catches the worm. The single most quoted German proverb about work.

**Example**:
"Ich stehe um fünf auf. Morgenstund hat Gold im Mund."
("I get up at five. The early bird catches the worm.")

### 2. Ohne Fleiß kein Preis

**Literal**: Without diligence, no prize.
**Meaning**: No pain, no gain. Said to children constantly.

**Example**:
"Du musst üben. Ohne Fleiß kein Preis."
("You have to practise. No pain, no gain.")

### 3. Übung macht den Meister

**Literal**: Practice makes the master.
**Meaning**: Practice makes perfect.

**Example**:
"Beim zehnten Mal klappt es. Übung macht den Meister."
("It works by the tenth try. Practice makes perfect.")

### 4. Erst die Arbeit, dann das Vergnügen

**Literal**: First the work, then the pleasure.
**Meaning**: Business before pleasure, and in Germany it is not a suggestion.

**Example**:
"Nach dem Bericht gehen wir. Erst die Arbeit, dann das Vergnügen."
("We'll go after the report. Business before pleasure.")

### 5. Was du heute kannst besorgen, das verschiebe nicht auf morgen

**Literal**: What you can take care of today, do not postpone to tomorrow.
**Meaning**: Never put off until tomorrow what you can do today.

**Example**:
"Mach es jetzt. Was du heute kannst besorgen, das verschiebe nicht auf morgen."
("Do it now. Don't put off until tomorrow what you can do today.")

### 6. Es ist noch kein Meister vom Himmel gefallen

**Literal**: No master has ever fallen from the sky.
**Meaning**: Nobody is born an expert. Used to reassure a beginner.

**Example**:
"Sei nicht so streng mit dir. Es ist noch kein Meister vom Himmel gefallen."
("Don't be so hard on yourself. Nobody was born knowing this.")

### 7. Wer den Pfennig nicht ehrt, ist des Talers nicht wert

**Literal**: He who does not honour the penny is not worthy of the taler.
**Meaning**: Look after the small amounts and the large ones look after themselves.

**Example**:
"Ich sammle die Pfandflaschen. Wer den Pfennig nicht ehrt, ist des Talers nicht wert."
("I collect the deposit bottles. Take care of the pennies.")

### 8. Viele Köche verderben den Brei

**Literal**: Many cooks spoil the porridge.
**Meaning**: Too many cooks spoil the broth.

**Example**:
"Wir sind zu viele an dem Projekt. Viele Köche verderben den Brei."
("There are too many of us on this project. Too many cooks.")

### 9. Wer A sagt, muss auch B sagen

**Literal**: He who says A must also say B.
**Meaning**: If you start something, finish it. Used when someone wants to abandon a commitment halfway.

**Example**:
"Du hast zugesagt. Wer A sagt, muss auch B sagen."
("You agreed to it. In for a penny, in for a pound.")

### 10. Aller Anfang ist schwer

**Literal**: Every beginning is hard.
**Meaning**: Starting is always the difficult part. Every German teacher says this in week one.

**Example**:
"Die Grammatik kommt noch. Aller Anfang ist schwer."
("The grammar will come. Every beginning is hard.")

### 11. Wer rastet, der rostet

**Literal**: He who rests, rusts.
**Meaning**: Keep moving or you decline. Said about bodies and about skills.

**Example**:
"Mein Opa läuft täglich. Wer rastet, der rostet."
("My grandfather walks every day. If you rest, you rust.")

### 12. Der Apfel fällt nicht weit vom Stamm

**Literal**: The apple does not fall far from the trunk.
**Meaning**: Like father, like son. Usually said with a raised eyebrow.

**Example**:
"Er ist genauso stur wie sein Vater. Der Apfel fällt nicht weit vom Stamm."
("He's as stubborn as his father. The apple doesn't fall far from the tree.")

## Proverbs About Life, Luck and Other People

### 13. Andere Länder, andere Sitten

**Literal**: Other countries, other customs.
**Meaning**: When in Rome. The polite way to accept that things are done differently here.

**Example**:
"Hier isst man um zwölf. Andere Länder, andere Sitten."
("People eat at twelve here. When in Rome.")

### 14. Wo ein Wille ist, ist auch ein Weg

**Literal**: Where there is a will, there is also a way.
**Meaning**: Identical to the English.

**Example**:
"Wir schaffen das schon. Wo ein Wille ist, ist auch ein Weg."
("We'll manage. Where there's a will, there's a way.")

### 15. Lügen haben kurze Beine

**Literal**: Lies have short legs.
**Meaning**: Lies do not get far before they are caught. Said to children above all.

**Example**:
"Sag die Wahrheit. Lügen haben kurze Beine."
("Tell the truth. Lies don't get far.")

### 16. Kleider machen Leute

**Literal**: Clothes make people.
**Meaning**: People judge you by how you look. Also the title of a famous novella, which keeps it in circulation.

**Example**:
"Zieh das Hemd an. Kleider machen Leute."
("Put the shirt on. Clothes make the man.")

### 17. Reden ist Silber, Schweigen ist Gold

**Literal**: Speaking is silver, silence is gold.
**Meaning**: Silence is golden. Very often deployed as a hint.

**Example**:
"Sag jetzt nichts. Reden ist Silber, Schweigen ist Gold."
("Don't say anything now. Silence is golden.")

### 18. Scherben bringen Glück

**Literal**: Broken shards bring luck.
**Meaning**: Breaking something by accident is good luck, which is why Germans smash crockery the night before a wedding.

**Example**:
"Das Glas ist runtergefallen. Scherben bringen Glück!"
("The glass fell. Broken glass brings luck!")

### 19. Alles hat ein Ende, nur die Wurst hat zwei

**Literal**: Everything has an end, only the sausage has two.
**Meaning**: Everything finishes eventually. A joke proverb, and genuinely used.

**Example**:
"Der Urlaub ist vorbei. Alles hat ein Ende, nur die Wurst hat zwei."
("The holiday is over. All good things come to an end.")

### 20. Der Teufel steckt im Detail

**Literal**: The devil is in the detail.
**Meaning**: The same as in English, and taken seriously.

**Example**:
"Der Vertrag sieht gut aus, aber der Teufel steckt im Detail."
("The contract looks fine, but the devil is in the detail.")

### 21. Man soll den Tag nicht vor dem Abend loben

**Literal**: One should not praise the day before the evening.
**Meaning**: Do not count your chickens before they hatch.

**Example**:
"Noch ist nichts unterschrieben. Man soll den Tag nicht vor dem Abend loben."
("Nothing is signed yet. Don't count your chickens.")

### 22. In der Kürze liegt die Würze

**Literal**: In brevity lies the spice.
**Meaning**: Keep it short. Brevity is the soul of wit.

**Example**:
"Halt die Rede kurz. In der Kürze liegt die Würze."
("Keep the speech short. Brevity is the spice of it.")

### 23. Jeder ist seines Glückes Schmied

**Literal**: Everyone is the blacksmith of their own fortune.
**Meaning**: You make your own luck.

**Example**:
"Sie hat es sich selbst aufgebaut. Jeder ist seines Glückes Schmied."
("She built it herself. You make your own luck.")

### 24. Was Hänschen nicht lernt, lernt Hans nimmermehr

**Literal**: What little Hans does not learn, grown Hans will never learn.
**Meaning**: What you fail to learn young is hard to pick up later. Often quoted at language learners, fairly or not.

**Example**:
"Er hat nie getippt gelernt. Was Hänschen nicht lernt, lernt Hans nimmermehr."
("He never learned to type. Old habits are hard to change.")

### 25. Es ist nicht alles Gold, was glänzt

**Literal**: Not all that glitters is gold.
**Meaning**: Identical to the English, and just as common.

**Example**:
"Das Angebot klingt zu gut. Es ist nicht alles Gold, was glänzt."
("The offer sounds too good. All that glitters is not gold.")

## What These Proverbs Say About the Culture

Line them up and a pattern appears. Of the 25 here, roughly half are about work, diligence, finishing what you started and not wasting small amounts of money. Almost none are about romance, which is the opposite of what you find in the French and Italian equivalents.

That is not an accident of selection. German proverbs cluster around effort and consequence because most of them were sharpened in a world of guilds, apprenticeships and craft. *Es ist noch kein Meister vom Himmel gefallen* is not a metaphor about masters, it is literally about the master craftsman rank. *Wer den Pfennig nicht ehrt* names two real coins.

The other thing to notice is how blunt they are. Lies have short legs. He who rests, rusts. Clothes make people. There is very little decoration, and a learner who tries to soften them in translation loses the point.

## How to Use Them Without Sounding Like a Textbook

- Deploy one per conversation at most. Proverbs land when they are rare, and stacking two in a row sounds like you memorised a list.
- The safest are the ones that match English exactly: *der Teufel steckt im Detail*, *wo ein Wille ist, ist auch ein Weg*, *es ist nicht alles Gold, was glänzt*. You already know when to use them.
- Be careful with *Was Hänschen nicht lernt*. Said to an adult learner it can sound discouraging, even when it was meant lightly.
