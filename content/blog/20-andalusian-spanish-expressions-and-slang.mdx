Andalusian Spanish is not Castilian spoken carelessly. It is a variety with its own rhythm, its own vowels and its own vocabulary, and **Andalusian Spanish expressions** are the fastest way to hear the difference. A speaker from Seville, Cadiz or Malaga can be understood anywhere in the Spanish speaking world and still say twenty things in an afternoon that a Madrileño would never say.

Below are 20 **Andalusian Spanish expressions** you will only hear in the south, each with its meaning, an example, and the English translation.

## How Andalusians Address Each Other

### 1. Quillo / illo
Short for *chiquillo*, and the classic Andalusian way to get someone's attention. It works like "mate" and appears at the start or the end of almost any sentence.

*Quillo, dónde te habías metido?* (Mate, where had you got to?)

### 2. Pisha
The Cadiz version of the same idea, used constantly between friends on the coast. Unmistakably *gaditano*.

*Vamos a la playa, pisha, que hace un día bueno.* (Let's go to the beach, mate, it's a lovely day.)

### 3. Miarma
From *mi alma*, "my soul". Seville's own term of affection, used with friends, strangers and shopkeepers alike.

*Ay, miarma, no te preocupes por eso.* (Oh love, don't worry about that.)

### 4. Compae
From *compadre*. Friend, mate, the person you are talking to right now.

*Échame una mano, compae.* (Give me a hand, mate.)

### 5. Malaje
Someone with bad energy, unpleasant or spiteful. One of the most Andalusian judgements you can pass on a person.

*No lo invites, es un malaje.* (Don't invite him, he's got a nasty streak.)

## Reactions and Exclamations

### 6. Ozú
From *Jesús*. Pure surprise, admiration or dismay, depending on how long you stretch it.

*Ozú, qué calor hace hoy.* (Wow, it's so hot today.)

### 7. Ea
The most flexible word in Andalusia. It means yes, fine, of course, there you go, or nothing much at all. It fills silence comfortably.

*Ea, pues nos vemos mañana.* (Right then, see you tomorrow.)

### 8. No ni ná
Three negatives that mean an emphatic yes. It is used to confirm something obvious, usually with irony.

*Que si me gusta el pescaíto? No ni ná.* (Do I like fried fish? You bet I do.)

### 9. Fatiguita
Literally a little fatigue, but it means embarrassment or tender pity. *Me da fatiguita* is what you say when something makes you cringe on someone else's behalf.

*Me da fatiguita pedirle dinero.* (I feel awkward asking him for money.)

### 10. Tela
A lot, a great deal. Often extended to *tela marinera* for emphasis.

*Ha llovido tela esta semana.* (It has rained a huge amount this week.)

## Describing People and Things

### 11. Chalao
Crazy, in the affectionate sense. From *chalado*, with the *d* dropped as Andalusian does.

*Está chalao, se ha comprado otra moto.* (He's mad, he's bought another motorbike.)

### 12. Jartible
Someone unbearable, tiring, who wears you out. From *harto*, fed up.

*Ese niño es jartible, no para quieto.* (That kid is exhausting, he never sits still.)

### 13. Una jartá
A huge amount. From *hartada*, and used for anything you can count or not.

*Había una jartá de gente en la feria.* (There were loads of people at the fair.)

### 14. Chuminá
A triviality, a silly little thing, something not worth the fuss.

*No te enfades por una chuminá.* (Don't get angry over something so silly.)

### 15. De perita
Perfect, just right, exactly what was needed.

*Me viene de perita que vengas el sábado.* (It suits me perfectly that you're coming on Saturday.)

### 16. Escacharrao
Broken, out of order, ruined. Another dropped *d*, from *escacharrado*.

*El aire acondicionado está escacharrao.* (The air conditioning is broken.)

## Practical Andalusian Vocabulary

### 17. Canina
Serious hunger. *Tener canina* is to be properly starving, not just peckish.

*Tengo una canina que me muero.* (I'm absolutely starving.)

### 18. Bulla
Hurry, rush. *Tener bulla* is to be in a hurry, and in Andalusia it is usually said by someone who is not.

*No tengas bulla, que hay tiempo.* (Don't rush, there's time.)

### 19. Aliquindoi
To be alert, to keep watch. The story goes that it comes from English dock workers in Cadiz being told to "look and do it", which is exactly the kind of origin Andalusia enjoys.

*Estate aliquindoi con el bolso.* (Keep an eye on your bag.)

### 20. Pescaíto
Small fried fish, and the whole social ritual around eating it. The dropped *d* is the point: *pescadito* would sound foreign in a Cadiz beach bar.

*Nos tomamos unas cervezas con pescaíto.* (We had some beers with fried fish.)

## The Sounds That Make These Words Work

Andalusian vocabulary and Andalusian pronunciation are not separable. Four habits explain most of what you will hear, and knowing them turns a wall of sound into words you already know.

**The disappearing *d*.** Between vowels it goes: *pescado* becomes *pescaíto*, *cansado* becomes *cansao*, *escacharrado* becomes *escacharrao*. This is why half the words on this list look shortened.

**The soft final *s*.** It becomes a breath rather than a letter, and the vowel before it opens up to carry the plural instead. *Los niños* sounds closer to *loh niñoh*, and the meaning lives in the vowel.

**Seseo and ceceo.** In most of Andalusia *c*, *z* and *s* collapse into a single sound. Which one depends on the town, sometimes on the neighbourhood, and locals can place each other by it instantly.

**Words that run together.** *Para el* becomes *pal*, *mi alma* becomes *miarma*, *chiquillo* becomes *quillo*. Several entries on this list are simply two words that stopped pretending to be separate.

## How to Sound Southern Without Overdoing It

**Do not fake the accent.** Andalusian drops final *s*, softens *ch*, and eats intervocalic *d*, but a learner performing it sounds like mockery. Learn to understand it first. The vocabulary is what marks you as someone who has actually been there.

**Understand *ustedes* in the south.** Much of western Andalusia uses *ustedes* where the rest of Spain uses *vosotros*, often with the *vosotros* verb form. It is not formality, it is the local plural.

**Diminutives are everywhere.** *Fatiguita*, *pescaíto*, *ratito*. In Andalusia the diminutive is not about size, it is about warmth and softening what you just said.

**Remember this is one region, not all of Spain.** These expressions will delight people in Seville and confuse people in Bilbao. That is exactly the point.
